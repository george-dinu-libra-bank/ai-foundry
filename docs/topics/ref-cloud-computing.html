Cloud computing — a general overview

What the cloud is, what it actually gives you compared to owning servers, and where it genuinely hurts.

A standalone reference for this course: read it once end to end, return to it whenever a session touches infrastructure. No Azure specifics here — this page is about the model itself, valid for any provider.

Reference page ~15 min read
The concept

What cloud computing is

Cloud computing is the delivery of computing capabilities — processing, storage, networking, databases, and increasingly entire application platforms — as metered services over a network, from shared pools of provider-operated infrastructure. The customer consumes capacity on demand and pays for what is used; the provider owns, operates and continuously renews the physical estate.

The definition most of the industry still anchors to comes from NIST (the US National Institute of Standards and Technology), which names five essential characteristics. They are worth knowing verbatim, because together they distinguish a real cloud from "a server in someone's rack":

CharacteristicMeaning
On-demand self-serviceYou provision capacity yourself, through a portal or API — no purchase order, no human in the loop.
Broad network accessEverything is reachable over standard networks and protocols, from any client platform.
Resource poolingThe provider serves many customers from shared physical infrastructure (multi-tenancy), assigning and reassigning capacity dynamically.
Rapid elasticityCapacity scales out and back quickly — from the consumer's perspective, it appears unlimited.
Measured serviceUsage is metered at fine granularity; you can observe it, and you are billed by it.

Note what the definition does not say: nothing about who owns the hardware being "someone else". The essence is the service model — self-service, pooled, elastic, metered — which is why large organizations can also run private clouds that satisfy every characteristic on their own premises.

The concept

How it's built: virtualization, multi-tenancy, hyperscale

The enabling technology is virtualization: a layer of software (the hypervisor) that presents one physical machine as many isolated virtual machines, each with its own virtual CPU, memory, disk and network. Virtualization decouples the logical unit a customer rents from the physical unit the provider operates — a VM can be created in seconds, resized, migrated between hosts, and destroyed without a human touching hardware. Containers push the same idea one level up: instead of virtualizing hardware, they isolate processes sharing one operating-system kernel, which makes them lighter and faster to start — the packaging unit of modern application deployment.

Multi-tenancy is the economic consequence: many customers' workloads share the same physical pool, isolated by the virtualization layer and by software-defined networking. Pooling is what makes elasticity affordable — individual customers' peaks and troughs average out, so the provider can run its fleet at high utilization while every tenant experiences apparently unlimited headroom.

Physically, the pool lives in hyperscale datacenters — purpose-built facilities measured in hundreds of megawatts, grouped into regions around the world, with redundant power, cooling and connectivity, joined by private fiber networks. Providers place regions to balance latency to population centers, energy economics, and jurisdiction — the last of which matters because where data physically resides determines which laws apply to it (data residency), a first-order concern for regulated industries such as banking.

The concept

The economics: from CAPEX to OPEX

Owning infrastructure is a capital game. You forecast demand years ahead, buy for the peak (plus safety margin), depreciate the hardware whether it is busy or idle, and staff the operation around the clock. Under-forecast and you hit a capacity wall at the worst moment; over-forecast and capital sits idle. This is CAPEX — capital expenditure — and its defining property is that the cost is committed before the demand is known.

The cloud converts that commitment into OPEX — operating expenditure that tracks actual consumption. Capacity follows demand instead of preceding it: a nightly batch job pays for its hours; a seasonal campaign pays for its season; an experiment that fails is switched off and costs nothing further. Just as important is the option value: trying an idea no longer requires a procurement cycle, which is why cloud adoption correlates so strongly with delivery speed.

Two honest footnotes. First, at very large, very steady scale, owning can be cheaper than renting — the cloud's premium buys elasticity and operations you may not need if your load is flat and predictable. Second, pay-per-use cuts both ways: waste also scales frictionlessly. Idle VMs, oversized databases and forgotten experiments bill by the hour, which is why mature organizations treat cost as an engineering metric (the practice called FinOps) — and why every exercise in this course reports what it cost.

The models

Service models: IaaS, PaaS, SaaS — and shared responsibility

Cloud services are classified by how much of the stack the provider manages. IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service) rents you the fundamentals — virtual machines, disks, networks — and everything from the operating system upward is yours to install, patch and secure. PaaS (Platform as a Service) rents you a running platform — "a database", "a container runtime", "a model endpoint" — and you bring only your application and data. SaaS (Software as a Service) rents you the finished application itself, configured rather than built.

The classification matters because it draws the shared-responsibility line: security and operations are always split between provider and customer, and the split moves with the service model. Read the matrix column by column — and notice the row that never changes hands: identity, access and your data are yours in every model. Most cloud breaches are not provider failures; they are customer misconfigurations on the customer's side of this line.

Identity & accessYouYouYouYou

A useful mental model: IaaS moves your machines to the cloud; PaaS moves your architecture; SaaS moves your process. Modern AI engineering is overwhelmingly a PaaS discipline — model endpoints, managed databases, container platforms — because the differentiating work is the application, not the plumbing underneath it.

The models

Deployment models: public, private, hybrid

Independent of what is offered is who may use the pool. The public cloud is the shared, provider-owned estate open to any customer — maximum elasticity and service breadth, the default for new work. A private cloud dedicates the pool to one organization (on its premises or hosted), trading scale for control — chosen where regulation, data sensitivity or latency demand it. In practice most large organizations, and virtually every bank, run a hybrid: core systems and their most sensitive data remain on-premises or private, while new capabilities — analytics, customer channels, AI — run in the public cloud, with controlled connectivity between the two. A related term you will hear, multi-cloud, simply means using more than one public provider — usually to avoid concentration risk, at the price of duplicated skills and tooling.

Scaling patterns

Two ways to grow. Vertical scaling (scale up) replaces the machine with a bigger one — simple, bounded by the biggest machine money rents, and briefly disruptive. Horizontal scaling (scale out) adds identical instances behind a load balancer — effectively unbounded and failure-tolerant, but it demands the application tier be stateless: any instance must be able to serve any request, which is exactly why state lives in the data layer of the anatomy above, never in the application's memory.

The trade-offs

What you gain

Elasticity and speed. Capacity in minutes instead of months, sized to demand in both directions. The organizational effect is larger than the technical one: experiments become cheap, and cheap experiments change how teams work.

Managed services. Databases that patch themselves, model endpoints that simply exist, platforms operated by the provider's specialists to a contractual SLA. Undifferentiated heavy lifting — backups, failover, certificate rotation — leaves your team's calendar.

Global reach and resilience. Deploying near your users, in any of dozens of regions, with zone- and region-level redundancy available as configuration rather than construction projects.

Access to frontier capability. The clearest AI-era gain: state-of-the-art models, GPU fleets and vector search engines are available per-call to a two-person team. The capital barrier that once separated large organizations from advanced computing has effectively dissolved — what remains is the skill to use it, which is what this course is for.

The trade-offs

What's challenging

Security and compliance stay yours. The provider's certifications cover their side of the shared-responsibility line; a world-class datacenter does not save a storage account left open to the internet. Regulated industries add residency, auditability and vendor-risk obligations that constrain which services, regions and configurations are permissible — in a bank, "can we use this service?" is a governance question before it is a technical one.

Cost requires governance. The meter never stops. Without ownership of the bill — budgets, alerts, tagging, regular review — elasticity produces surprise invoices instead of savings. Treat cost as a first-class system metric, exactly like latency or error rate.

Lock-in and data gravity. The most productive services are the most proprietary — the deeper you integrate a provider's platform, the more an exit costs. Data compounds this: large datasets are slow and expensive to move (egress is billed), and workloads gravitate to where the data is. The mature posture is not avoiding lock-in at all costs — that forfeits most of the cloud's value — but knowing, per system, what an exit would take.

New failure modes and skills. Distributed systems fail in distributed ways: throttling, transient faults, regional incidents. Teams must learn to design for retry and degradation, and operations shifts from racking hardware to governing configuration — a genuine reskilling, not a simplification.
